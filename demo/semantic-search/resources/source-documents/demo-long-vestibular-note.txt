REASON FOR VISIT
Elena returns for evaluation of a second kind of dizziness that feels different from the lightheaded episodes she has described after standing. The purpose of this visit is to distinguish a brief motion-triggered spinning sensation from her prior near-fainting history and to document the response to bedside treatment. She attended with her daughter and provided a clear, internally consistent history.

HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS
About three weeks ago Elena began noticing a sudden sensation that the room moved when she rolled onto her right side in bed. The first event occurred while reaching toward the nightstand after turning her head. The spinning was intense but lasted less than one minute. It stopped when she remained still. Similar episodes occurred when she looked upward to place dishes on a shelf, bent to tie a shoe, or turned quickly toward the right while lying down. Between events she felt mildly cautious but did not have continuous vertigo.

She carefully distinguishes these events from the washed-out feeling that sometimes follows standing in a warm room. During the new events she did not experience gray vision, sweating, generalized weakness, chest pressure, palpitations, or a sense that she would lose consciousness. She did not faint. Her daughter did not observe unresponsiveness, confusion, abnormal movements, facial asymmetry, or speech change. There was no new hearing loss, ear pressure, pulsatile tinnitus, severe headache, double vision, limb incoordination, or focal numbness.

Elena tried rising more slowly and drinking extra water. Those measures helped her general confidence but did not prevent the brief spinning caused by rolling in bed. She stopped sleeping on the right side for several nights and used two pillows. She has not fallen. She continues to prepare meals, manage medications, and walk around her home without an assistive device. She avoided driving on mornings when she felt uncertain.

RELEVANT BACKGROUND
The chart contains a prior emergency evaluation for positional lightheadedness during reduced oral intake. Cardiac monitoring at that time did not show a sustained arrhythmia, and the neurologic examination was nonfocal. That history remains clinically important, but Elena says the current symptom has a different quality, a shorter duration, and a reproducible head-motion trigger. She has no history of vestibular neuritis, Meniere disease, ear surgery, migraine aura, stroke, or seizure. She has hypertension and type 2 diabetes. There has been no recent dose increase in a sedating medication.

EXAMINATION
Seated blood pressure was 132/76 mmHg with pulse 72. After standing for three minutes, blood pressure was 124/72 mmHg with pulse 78, and the maneuver did not reproduce her spinning. Mental status, language, visual fields, pupils, facial movement, limb strength, sensation, and finger-to-nose testing were normal. Gait was cautious for the first few steps but became steady. There was no spontaneous nystagmus in primary gaze. Head impulse testing did not show a corrective saccade. Hearing was symmetric to conversation.

With the Dix-Hallpike maneuver to the right, Elena developed a short latency followed by upbeat torsional nystagmus and the same room-spinning sensation she experiences in bed. Symptoms and nystagmus fatigued within approximately twenty-five seconds. The left Dix-Hallpike maneuver was negative. There was no direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus and no new neurologic deficit during testing.

TREATMENT AND RESPONSE
The pattern was consistent with right posterior canal benign paroxysmal positional vertigo. A right-sided Epley canalith-repositioning maneuver was performed in the clinic. Elena tolerated each position without nausea or vomiting. Repeat right Dix-Hallpike testing produced only two seconds of mild disequilibrium and no visible nystagmus. She walked the hallway afterward without assistance and said the room no longer shifted when she turned toward the right.

ASSESSMENT
1. Right posterior canal benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, supported by a characteristic positional trigger and fatigable torsional nystagmus.
2. Separate history of orthostatic lightheadedness or presyncope, not reproduced today and not the explanation for the brief spinning events described at this visit.
3. No examination findings suggesting an acute central vestibular syndrome, stroke, sustained arrhythmia, or seizure.

PLAN
Elena and her daughter were taught a home right-sided Epley maneuver using written instructions. She may repeat it once daily for up to three days if the same brief positional spinning returns. Vestibular suppressants are not recommended because the attacks are short and because sedation could increase fall risk. She should continue normal hydration and change positions deliberately, but prolonged activity restriction is unnecessary. A vestibular therapy referral is available if symptoms recur or home treatment is difficult.

Return precautions were reviewed. She should seek urgent assessment for continuous severe vertigo, inability to walk, new weakness, new speech or vision change, a sudden severe headache, chest pain, sustained palpitations, actual loss of consciousness, or an injury-producing fall. A routine follow-up call is planned in two weeks. The patient accurately repeated the distinction between brief head-motion spinning and near-fainting after standing.
